The Greenville Stability Framework

A Municipal Housing and Food Security Whitepaper
Pilot Jurisdiction: Greenville
Operating Within the Legal Framework of New Hampshire

1. Introduction
Across the United States, small municipalities face a common structural challenge:
• Housing is financed through compounding debt.
• Property tax volatility destabilizes households.
• Food supply chains externalize profit.
• Local revenue leaks outward.
• Municipal budgets remain dependent on unstable tax flows.
These dynamics are not the result of local failure.
They are structural features of modern financial architecture.
The Greenville Stability Framework proposes a lawful, voluntary, and fiscally responsible municipal strategy to:
• Stabilize residential mortgage obligations
• Integrate property tax predictably
• Strengthen local food production and distribution
• Reduce household volatility
• Improve long-term municipal credit stability
This proposal does not abolish markets.
It does not confiscate property.
It does not eliminate tax obligations.
It restructures financial leverage to improve local resilience.

2. Structural Problem Statement

2.1 Mortgage Compounding Risk
Residential mortgages are typically structured with:
• Long-term interest compounding
• Escrow-based tax volatility
• Foreclosure-trigger enforcement
While legally valid, this structure introduces:
• Household stress volatility
• Default cascades during economic downturns
• Foreclosure externalities that depress property values
• Municipal revenue instability
Foreclosure is not merely a private event.
It is a systemic destabilizer.

2.2 Property Tax Dependence in New Hampshire
New Hampshire relies heavily on property tax revenue for:
• Municipal operations
• School funding
• County obligations
• State education assessments
This creates:
• High sensitivity to property value fluctuations
• Budget volatility
• Reassessment-driven stress events
Eliminating property taxes outright would violate state funding structures.
Therefore, the objective is stabilization — not removal.

2.3 Food Supply Leakage
In many small towns:
• Grocery profits leave the municipality.
• Local farms sell into volatile external markets.
• Residents spend a large portion of income outside town.
This reduces local economic velocity and weakens fiscal resilience.

3. Core Design Principles
The Framework rests on five principles:
• Ownership Preservation
• Voluntary Participation
• Legal Compliance
• Transparency
• Non-Extractive Public Design
These principles ensure the model remains lawful, ethical, and politically sustainable.

4. Greenville Municipal Housing Trust (GMHT)

4.1 Purpose
The GMHT shall function as a public-benefit municipal trust designed to:
• Purchase and restructure residential mortgage debt
• Stabilize housing obligations
• Improve municipal credit reliability
• Reduce foreclosure incidence

4.2 Legal Basis
Under New Hampshire municipal authority:
• Municipalities may issue bonds.
• Public-benefit trusts may be established.
• Mortgage notes are transferable financial instruments.
The GMHT operates within these existing authorities.
No contracts are voided.
Mortgages are purchased at negotiated market rates.

4.3 Mortgage Restructuring Terms
Upon acquisition, mortgages are restructured as:
• 0–1% administrative servicing rate
• Principal-only amortization
• No securitization resale
• Hardship deferral protocols
• Foreclosure as last-resort mechanism only
The homeowner retains:
• Full title
• Renovation rights
• Sale rights
• Inheritance rights
• Appreciation gains
The Trust holds only the debt instrument, not property ownership.

5. Property Tax Integration

5.1 Structural Absorption
Property taxes remain legally required.
For participating households:
• Taxes are embedded within a single stabilized monthly obligation.
• The Trust remits required amounts to state, county, and school authorities.
• Volatility smoothing mechanisms prevent sudden reassessment shocks.
The resident experiences:
Predictability.
The state experiences:
Uninterrupted compliance.

5.2 Stabilization Reserve
A portion of Trust revenue shall fund a Property Tax Stabilization Reserve to:
• Buffer reassessment spikes
• Cover temporary delinquencies
• Maintain bond rating integrity

6. Transfer & Exit Framework

6.1 Upon Sale
When a Trust-participating home is sold:
• Outstanding principal is paid at closing.
• Seller retains appreciation.
• Buyer is offered Trust financing first.

6.2 Exit Rights
Participants may refinance privately.
However, exit results in:
• Loss of stabilization benefits
• Loss of contribution offsets
• Loss of dividend participation
• Re-entry waiting period
• Stabilization fee (nominal)
Exit remains lawful.
Participation remains economically superior.

7. Civic Contribution Credit System (CCCS)
The CCCS allows residents to contribute time and skills toward:
• Agricultural production
• Grocery operations
• Infrastructure maintenance
• Civic services
Credits:
• Are non-transferable
• Decay over time
• May offset portions of housing obligation
• May be used for essential groceries
Credits do not purchase ownership.
They supplement participation.

8. Greenville Food Stability Agreement (GFSA)

8.1 Structure
The GMHT enters a Public-Private Stabilization Agreement with a locally owned grocery.
The grocery:
• Remains privately owned and managed.
• Receives expansion capital.
• Accepts Contribution Credits for essentials.
• Shares expansion-driven profits with the Municipal Debt Stabilization Reserve.

8.2 Farm Integration
Participating farms receive:
• Guaranteed purchase contracts
• Labor supplementation via CCCS
• Predictable demand floor
• Reduced distribution risk
Farm autonomy is preserved.

8.3 Economic Loop Effect
Housing stability increases disposable income.
Disposable income increases local grocery demand.
Grocery profitability strengthens municipal debt reserve.
Debt reserve strengthens bond rating.
Lower interest improves housing affordability.
Positive reinforcement cycle.

9. Governance & Oversight
The Framework includes:
• Rotating Civic Oversight Council
• Independent audits
• Quarterly public reporting
• Conflict-of-interest disclosures
• 10-year sunset review clause
Emergency powers auto-expire.
Power does not accumulate.

• Issue general obligation bonds

Housing stabilization and foreclosure prevention fall within recognized public-purpose categories under municipal finance doctrine.
The proposed bond issuance would:
• Be voter-approved (as required)
• Follow statutory debt limits
• Undergo independent financial review
• Be publicly disclosed
No extraordinary powers are required.

Residential mortgages are transferable financial instruments.
Financial institutions routinely:
• Sell mortgage notes
• Bundle and securitize debt
• Transfer servicing rights
The GMHT would:
• Negotiate voluntary purchase of mortgage notes
• Acquire debt at agreed market value
• Modify terms upon acquisition
This does not:
• Void contracts
• Seize assets
• Impair lender rights without compensation
It operates through lawful purchase.

12.3 Public-Benefit Trust Structure
Municipalities may create public-benefit entities to:
• Manage infrastructure
• Operate utilities
• Administer public financing programs
The GMHT functions similarly to:
• Housing authorities

Its role is financial stabilization, not land accumulation.

12.4 Property Tax Compliance
Property taxes in New Hampshire fund:
• Municipal budgets
• County obligations
• State education tax
The Framework does not eliminate property tax obligations.
It restructures:
Collection method and payment timing.
The Trust remits:
Full required amounts to appropriate authorities.
Thus:
State and county compliance remains intact.
No statutory conflict arises.

12.5 Public-Private Grocery Partnership Legality
Municipalities may enter into:
• Public-private partnerships
• Economic development agreements
• Infrastructure improvement contracts
The Greenville Food Stability Agreement:
• Does not nationalize private business

12.6 Constitutional Considerations
The Framework respects:

Because:

There is no compelled transfer of ownership.

12.7 Federal Compliance Considerations
The Framework does not:
• Alter federal mortgage underwriting standards
• Interfere with federal housing programs
• Violate Fair Housing Act provisions
• Impair interstate commerce
It functions at municipal financial level only.

12.8 Debt Limit & Rating Considerations
Prior to issuance:

Section 13 – Multi-Stream Municipal Revenue Architecture

The Stabilization Layering Model

13.1 Foundational Premise

The Greenville Stabilization Framework does not rely on singular revenue transformation or abrupt systemic disruption.

Instead, it implements a phased, multi-stream revenue architecture designed to:
• Internalize local economic value.
• Reduce volatility exposure.
• Capitalize a Housing Stabilization Fund.
• Gradually compress mortgage dependence.
• Preserve legal compliance at all state and federal levels.

The objective is not immediate mortgage elimination, but structural principal compression through compounding municipal revenue generation.

13.2 Layered Revenue Structure

The model operates across six cumulative layers:

Phase I – Housing Stabilization (Voluntary Opt-In)
• Establish Greenville Municipal Housing Trust (GMHT)
• Issue compliant municipal bonds
• Begin structured housing covenant model

Phase II – Food & Agricultural Loop
• Local grocery partnership
• Local farm production agreements
• Profit redirection into Housing Stabilization Fund (HSF)

Phase III – Commercial Stabilization Zone (Primary Growth Lever)
• Route 31 corridor development
• Revenue participation model
• Export-oriented small business recruitment

Phase IV – Value-Add Local Production
• Processing, fabrication, artisan production
• Retention of export margin within municipality

Phase V – Remote Workforce Magnetization
• Attract location-independent professionals
• Offer stabilized housing + low-cost living
• Encourage voluntary civic participation contributions

Phase VI – Energy Micro-Grid Development
• Solar and distributed generation
• Utility revenue participation
• Long-term stable income layer
Each layer is independent but reinforcing.
Failure of one does not collapse the system.

13.3 Revenue Participation vs. Taxation

The Framework distinguishes between:
• Mandatory taxation
• Voluntary structured revenue participation

Revenue participation applies only to:
• Stabilized commercial corridor participants
• Covenant-bound housing resales
• Voluntary civic ledger engagement

Property tax obligations remain intact unless lawfully modified through state processes.

The system operates within compliance — not in opposition to federal or state authority.

Within Greenville, local agricultural integration offers:
• Predictable demand

• Community engagement
• Recirculating capital
Food is not ideological.

• Workforce support through civic contribution system
Partner Agreement:

• Dedicate agreed profit share to Housing Stabilization Fund

This is not municipal takeover.
It is partnership.

14.3 Agricultural Participation

Local farms are offered:
• Forward purchasing agreements
• Predictable demand contracts
• Infrastructure coordination support
• Optional participation in revenue participation model

Food loop effect:

Resident purchases → Grocery margin → Housing Fund

Farm sales → Local retention → Economic stability

Worker contribution credits → Reduced household burden

The food layer builds trust before larger commercial expansion.

14.4 Risk Controls
To avoid distortion:

• Transparent profit reporting
Food loop must remain competitive and voluntary.

Section 15 – Route 31 Stabilized Development Corridor

15.1 Geographic Rationale
Route 31 within Greenville presents:
• Underutilized frontage
• High visibility
• Expansion potential
• Limited displacement concerns
It is suitable for forward-oriented economic expansion rather than legacy disruption.

15.2 Stabilized Development Corridor (SDC)
The SDC is a designated commercial zone offering long-term cost stabilization in exchange for structured revenue participation.
It is governed through:
• Publicly disclosed participation contracts
• Independent oversight review
• Conflict-of-interest safeguards
• Sunset review provisions (10-year intervals)

15.3 Business Incentive Structure
Participating firms receive:
• 15–20 year stabilized lease terms
• Below-market commercial occupancy
• Infrastructure facilitation
• Civic workforce integration
• Reduced volatility exposure

In exchange:

They agree to:
• 4–6% revenue participation above defined baseline threshold
• Transparent accounting
• Local hiring preference (incentivized, not mandatory)
• Anti-coercion employment compliance

15.4 Revenue Formula
R_p = max(0, α × (Gross Revenue − Baseline Threshold))
Where: α = participation rate (0.04–0.06)
This protects startups and scales municipal revenue only when success is demonstrated.

15.5 Residential Expansion Integration
New housing constructed under GMHT structure includes:
• Material cost
• Labor cost
• Bond servicing allocation
• Reserve allocation
Excludes speculative markup.
Upon resale:
• Participation contribution returns capital to HSF.
• Covenant remains attached to property.
This creates a revolving housing capitalization loop.

15.6 Long-Term Objective
As SDC revenue compounds:
• Housing Fund grows.
• Bond principal compresses.
• Mortgage duration shortens.
• New homes require smaller financing loads.
The elimination of long-term mortgage dependency becomes an emergent property of compounding stabilization — not an imposed mandate.

15.7 Ethical and Legal Guardrails
The Framework explicitly prohibits:
• Mandatory enrollment
• Confiscation
• Exclusion of non-participants
• Interference with existing private contracts

All participation is voluntary and incentive-based.

The model operates within existing New Hampshire municipal law.

Section 21 – Surplus Protection & Resident Dividend Mandate
Anti-Extraction Legislative Safeguards

21.1 Foundational Principle

The Greenville Stabilization Framework is designed to reduce economic extraction — not relocate it from private lenders to municipal political actors.

Therefore:

No surplus revenue generated by the Stabilization Framework may be diverted into discretionary political compensation or unrelated municipal spending.

All net surplus must return to residents.
This principle is structural, not optional.

21.2 Definition of Surplus

For purposes of governance:
Net Surplus (NS) =

Total Stabilization Fund Revenue
− Bond Servicing Obligations
− Reserve Requirements
− Administrative Operating Costs (capped)
− Legally Required Municipal Allocations

Only after these obligations are satisfied may surplus be distributed or reinvested.

21.3 Administrative Cost Cap

To prevent bureaucratic expansion:
Administrative overhead for the Housing Stabilization Fund and associated entities shall not exceed:

5–8% of annual fund inflow (exact percentage to be voter-approved).

No performance bonuses tied to fund growth.

No elected official compensation linked to revenue expansion.

Oversight board members receive fixed stipends only.

21.4 Surplus Lock Provision

All Net Surplus must be allocated exclusively toward one or more of the following:
• Principal compression on remaining housing bonds.
• Direct mortgage reduction grants to covenant participants.
• Property tax relief offset pool.
• Infrastructure improvements benefiting all residents.
• Expansion of commercial corridor stabilization.
• Energy micro-grid deployment.
• Reserve strengthening.

Prohibited Uses:
• Salary increases for elected officials
• Pension expansions beyond standard municipal formula
• Political campaign financing
• Non-transparent discretionary allocations

This creates a structural firewall.

21.5 Resident Dividend Mechanism (Optional Advanced Phase)

Once:
• All housing bonds are fully serviced
• Required reserves exceed threshold
• Long-term obligations are stabilized

The town may vote to implement a Resident Dividend Distribution Model.

Possible structures:

A) Equal Per-Resident Dividend
B) Housing-Weighted Dividend
C) Property Tax Offset Distribution
D) Infrastructure Credit Allocation

The method must be approved by supermajority public vote.

21.6 Democratic Allocation Requirement

If Net Surplus exceeds a defined sustainability threshold for two consecutive fiscal years:

The town must hold a public vote determining allocation priorities for the upcoming cycle.

Options presented must be:
• Publicly disclosed
• Financially modeled
• Independently audited
• Legally compliant

No closed executive allocation authority.

21.7 Anti-Capture Safeguards

To prevent political capture:
• Mandatory independent annual audit.
• Publicly accessible real-time financial dashboard.
• Conflict-of-interest disclosure for all board members.
• Term limits for oversight roles.
• Citizen petition mechanism to trigger review.

21.8 Long-Term Structural Effect

This provision ensures:

The system cannot become a revenue pump for political elites.

If everyone eventually opts in and mortgages shrink toward zero:

Surplus capital cycles back to residents.
This preserves moral legitimacy.

21.9 Ethical Statement

The Greenville Stabilization Framework exists to:

Lower burdens. Increase agency. Strengthen resilience.

It does not exist to:

Accumulate municipal power. Concentrate political wealth. Create permanent administrative expansion.

If surplus emerges, it must decentralize.
